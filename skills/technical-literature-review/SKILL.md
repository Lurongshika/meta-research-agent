# Technical Literature Review

Build a verifiable map of a field rather than a list of plausible-looking citations.

## Scope and search

Translate the question into concepts, synonyms, seminal terminology, recent terminology, and exclusion criteria. Choose sources appropriate to the field: publisher libraries and indexing services for archival work; arXiv and conference proceedings for fast-moving areas; MathSciNet or zbMATH when available for mathematics; citation graphs for backward and forward chaining.

Record each database, complete query, filters, and search date. Search current authoritative sources when recency matters. Treat search snippets and secondary summaries as discovery aids, not evidence for technical claims.

## Verify and classify

Open the primary source before citing it. Verify title, authors, venue, year, version, DOI or stable identifier, and that the cited passage actually supports the claim. Distinguish peer-reviewed papers, preprints, technical reports, documentation, and informal posts.

Organize papers by problem formulation, assumptions, method family, evidence, limitations, and relationship to the target question. In ML, record datasets, splits, baselines, metrics, and compute. In systems, record workloads and environments. In mathematics, record theorem assumptions and dependency chains.

## Synthesize

Compare claims under compatible assumptions and evaluation conditions. Do not rank methods from incomparable tables. Identify consensus, contradictions, missing comparisons, negative results, and open problems. Make it explicit when a conclusion is an inference across sources rather than a statement made by a source.

Never invent citations, identifiers, quotations, or access dates. If a source cannot be opened, say so and limit the claim accordingly.

## Deliverables

Provide the scope, reproducible search log, inclusion logic, a structured evidence table, thematic synthesis, disagreements or gaps, and a verified bibliography with stable links or identifiers.
